Introduction
Diabetes is a major chronic condition affecting hundreds of millions globally and driving substantial healthcare burden. Early identification of individuals at high risk for diabetes and effective management of diagnosed patients are critical for preventing complications and reducing healthcare utilization. This project focuses on two interconnected but distinct predictive problems: identifying clinical and demographic predictors of diabetes in the general population and identifying predictors of 30-day hospital readmission among patients already diagnosed with diabetes. These goals reflect both prevention and disease-management priorities in public health.
The first objective leverages a large, diverse Kaggle clinical dataset to determine which measurable factors most strongly distinguish individuals with diabetes from those without. The second objective draws on the U.S. hospital readmission dataset from the UCI Machine Learning Repository to assess whether commonly recorded care-utilization variables can reliably predict early rehospitalization-an important but difficult-to-manage quality metric.
Together, these analyses help clarify what drives diabetes onset versus challenges in ongoing diabetes management.
Background & Significance
Hospital readmission among diabetic patients is a major concern: repeated admissions contribute substantially to healthcare costs, morbidity, and complications. Prior work has shown that poor glycemic control, insufficient monitoring, and inadequate transitions of care contribute to readmission risk. For example, the original study introducing the 130-US Hospitals dataset found that measurement of HbA1c during hospitalization was infrequent (~ 18.4%) and that readmission risk varied significantly depending on whether HbA1c was measured.
On the other hand, early detection of undiagnosed diabetes (or pre-diabetes) in outpatient or general clinical populations is critical for initiating lifestyle or medical interventions before complications arise. Large-scale efforts to predict diabetes based on clinical risk factors can inform public health screening strategies and individual risk profiling.
Advances in machine learning over the past decade — including non-linear models, regularization, feature engineering, and ensemble methods — have made it feasible to develop predictive models with reasonable accuracy and interpretability. These models can help identify high-risk patients automatically, potentially guiding clinical decisions or resource allocation. Indeed, recent work has demonstrated success in readmission prediction using both traditional ML and deep-learning methods on the 130-US dataset.
The 130-US Hospitals dataset was first publicly released by Beata Strack et al. (2014), who used multivariable logistic regression to examine the association between inpatient HbA1c measurement and 30-day readmission, controlling for demographics, admission type, comorbidities, and other covariates.
More recent studies have applied advanced machine-learning models (e.g., random forests, gradient boosting, embedding-based deep networks) to this dataset for readmission prediction. For instance, embedding-based deep learning models reportedly achieved AUC-ROC as high as 0.97 under three-class readmission classification (>30 days, <30 days, no readmission).
Simultaneously, there is growing research on predicting diabetes risk in broader populations using ML, combining demographic data, metabolic markers (e.g., HbA1c, blood glucose, BMI), lifestyle factors (e.g., smoking), and comorbidities. A recently published comparative study (2025) highlights the potential of hybrid architectures combining traditional ML, ensemble methods, and deep learning to reach high predictive accuracy for diabetes diagnosis.
Therefore, our dual-dataset study aligns with current research trends, and aims to replicate and extend previous findings, while providing a transparent, reproducible workflow that can be evaluated at a high level.

Methods
For diabetes diagnosis (Kaggle), the key factors are glycemic markers (HbA1c, blood glucose) plus age, BMI, hypertension, and heart disease.
For hospital readmission (UCI), the main predictors are treatment intensity / utilization measures: number of medications, time in hospital, and number of diagnoses / prior inpatient visits.
Because the two datasets capture different aspects of care, there is no single variable directly shared and strongly predictive in both models. The overlap is more conceptual. In both datasets, markers of disease severity and complexity (poor glycemic control, multiple diagnoses, many medications, longer stays) are associated with worse outcomes. However, given the weak performance of the readmission models, we cannot claim strong evidence of common risk factors driving both diagnosis and readmission only that more complex, poorly controlled disease seems to matter in both contexts. Overall, we find suggestive but not definitive overlap in risk factors due to data limitations and poor readmission predictability.
Given the nature of the two prediction problems (readmission within 30 days; diabetes status), we framed both as binary classification tasks. For each dataset, we applied the following modeling pipeline:
1. Exploratory Data Analysis (EDA) – summary statistics, distributions, missing data assessment, feature distributions, class imbalance check.
2. Preprocessing – data cleaning, encoding, normalization, and feature engineering (including nonlinear transformations and interaction terms).
3. Model training – baseline logistic regression, followed by more flexible models (e.g., Random Forest, Gradient Boosting) to accommodate nonlinear relationships and interactions.
4. Model evaluation – using stratified cross-validation (e.g., 5-fold), evaluating performance via metrics including accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, and area under the ROC curve (AUC-ROC). Given class imbalance (particularly readmission may be relatively rare), also consider precision-recall curves.

Top 15 Predictor Importance
The Top 15 Predictor Importance (from Random Forest diabetes model) tells us how much each variable contributed to the model’s predictive ability — and importantly, it provides insight into what drives diabetes risk in this dataset.
The Top 15 predictor importance ranking reveals that glycemic control measures dominate diabetes prediction. HbA1c level emerges as the single most powerful predictor because it directly reflects chronic blood-sugar elevation — the clinical hallmark of diabetes. Blood glucose level follows closely, confirming its role in detecting current hyperglycemia. These two features alone contribute most of the predictive signal in the model, demonstrating that diabetes classification is driven primarily by physiological evidence of impaired glucose regulation.
Age and BMI also appear as strong contributors. Older adults and individuals with higher body mass index have well-documented increased diabetes risk, consistent with obesity-related insulin resistance and declining pancreatic function with age. Chronic health conditions such as hypertension and heart disease show moderate importance, indicating that cardiometabolic comorbidities provide useful secondary insight into disease presence. Beyond these core clinical variables, demographic variables contribute very little predictive value. Factors like gender and ethnicity do not meaningfully improve prediction when strong biological indicators (HbA1c and glucose) are available. This suggests that demographic patterns of diabetes are largely mediated through underlying clinical risk factors, not
through inherently differential disease mechanisms in the dataset. Overall, the Top 15 importance results demonstrate that diabetes prediction is best informed by objective biomarkers and basic clinical health status, while medical history and demographics add only marginal benefits. This aligns directly with current clinical practice: screening guidelines are based on glucose measurements, weight, age, and cardiometabolic health — the very risk factors highlighted by the model.

Non-Linear Transformation in Regression Model
Non-Linear model for blood glucose squared
The OLS regression examines how several clinical variables relate to blood glucose levels and shows that some predictors are statistically significant while others contribute little additional information. The
model explains about 6% of the variation in glucose (R-squared = 0.060), indicating that while these variables are related to glucose, many other factors are also involved. BMI, hypertension, heart disease, and both the linear and squared HbA1c terms are significant and positively associated with higher glucose, suggesting that individuals with these conditions tend to have elevated levels. The negative coefficient for the linear HbA1c term combined with a positive coefficient for its squared term indicates a non-linear, curved relationship rather than a simple straight-line effect. In contrast, the squared BMI term is not statistically significant, meaning that adding the squared form of BMI does not meaningfully improve prediction beyond the linear BMI measure. The very large condition number suggests multicollinearity, likely because squared terms are correlated with their original variables, which can make coefficient estimates less stable. Overall, the results indicate meaningful associations between clinical risk factors and blood glucose, but they also show that the model captures only a small part of the total variability.
Non-linear terms to Time in Hospital regression
This OLS regression examines which factors are associated with the number of days a patient spends in the hospital, and the results indicate that several visit-history variables have statistically strong effects.
Overall, the model explains about 23% of the variation in time in hospital (R-squared = 0.233), which is a moderate level of explanatory power for observational health data. The number of medications, inpatient visits, and number of diagnoses all have positive and significant coefficients, meaning patients who take more medications, have more previous inpatient encounters, or have more diagnoses tend to stay longer. In contrast, previous outpatient or emergency visits have negative coefficients, suggesting that patients with more activity in those settings may require shorter hospital stays once admitted. Both squared terms—num_medications_sq and number_inpatient_sq—are negative and significant, indicating diminishing returns, or a curved relationship in which the effect of very high medication counts or inpatient history grows more slowly or eventually levels off. The large condition number signals potential multicollinearity due to including squared predictors, but the overall pattern suggests that patients with heavier medication use and more complex medical histories generally remain hospitalized longer.
Results
Across the two clinical datasets, predictive performance differed substantially depending on the outcome of interest. In the diabetes dataset, both logistic regression and random forest models achieved very strong discrimination, with AUC values close to 0.96, indicating reliable identification of diabetes status using clinical variables such as HbA1c, blood glucose, BMI, age, hypertension, and heart disease. In contrast, hospital readmission models trained on the UCI dataset showed only modest predictive ability. The logistic regression ROC curve produced an AUC of approximately 0.63, demonstrating limited accuracy in identifying patients who would be readmitted within 30 days. Feature importance from the random forest model showed that medication counts, length of stay, prior inpatient encounters, and number of diagnoses were most influential, but despite these associations, predictive performance remained weak. Non-linear OLS models additionally showed meaningful relationships between clinical variables and both blood glucose levels and time in hospital, including significant curved effects from polynomial terms, although the overall variance explained was relatively small. Taken together, these results suggest that diabetes onset is highly predictable using common clinical measures, whereas short-term readmission is considerably more difficult to model using standard administrative variables alone.
Conclusion
This project set out to answer three related questions regarding predictors of diabetes and predictors of hospital readmission in diabetic patients. For diabetes risk in the general population, we found strong and clinically expected results: HbA1c, blood glucose, age, BMI, hypertension, and heart disease were the primary drivers of diagnosis. The models performed very well, suggesting these common clinical measures provide sufficient information for reliable screening and identification efforts.
For 30-day readmission, however, we learned that prediction is far more challenging. Variables typically recorded in hospital administrative systems—medication counts, length of stay, prior admissions—offered only weak predictive insight. Even the best model performed little better than chance, meaning more comprehensive clinical and socioeconomic data would be needed for actionable readmission risk stratification.
Regarding whether shared systemic risk factors influence both outcomes, we observed only broad thematic overlap: patients with more severe or complex disease tend to show worse outcomes in both
settings. However, due to the minimal predictive strength of the readmission models, this cannot be interpreted as strong evidence of common drivers.
Overall, the findings emphasize that diabetes onset can be predicted well through biological markers, while hospital readmission cannot be predicted effectively from limited utilization-based data. Future work should incorporate richer clinical details, medication adherence data, and social determinants of health to create more robust predictive models that could improve care coordination and reduce avoidable hospitalizations.
